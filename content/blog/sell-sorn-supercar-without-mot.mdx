Yes, you can sell a supercar that is declared SORN and has no current MOT. You need a buyer willing to take it in that condition and a suitable collection arrangement. Getting it running and tested is not a prerequisite for asking us for an offer.

Perhaps the car went into storage after a fault, or a repair project stopped and the MOT expired. Start with its condition today. An old MOT, an earlier diagnosis or the fact that it drove into the garage does not tell a buyer whether it can move now.

## SORN, an expired MOT and a non-runner are different

SORN is an administrative status, not a mechanical diagnosis. Under [GOV.UK's SORN guidance](https://www.gov.uk/sorn-statutory-off-road-notification), the vehicle must be kept off public roads, for example on a drive, in a garage or on private land.

A car can be SORN while still having an MOT. It can also have an expired MOT but no confirmed mechanical fault. A non-runner describes its inability to run, regardless of either document.

Explain all three separately in your enquiry: the current tax or SORN status, the MOT position and what you know about the vehicle's operation. This prevents a buyer interpreting “no MOT” as “failed its MOT” when it simply has not been presented for a test.

## You do not need to arrange a road test for a buyer

A SORN car cannot be taken on an ordinary public-road test drive. GOV.UK limits road use while SORN to journeys to or from a pre-booked MOT or other testing appointment. That exception is not a general permission to drive it to a purchaser.

An MOT exemption for a particular journey does not remove the need to consider the vehicle's actual safety and insurance. For a broken car being sold as it stands, arrange professional transport instead of trying to fit the collection around an exception.

[GOV.UK's MOT guidance](https://www.gov.uk/getting-an-mot) explains the restrictions when a test has expired. This guide concerns sales and collection in Great Britain; Northern Ireland has a separate testing process.

## Describe what has changed during storage

Tell the buyer when the car last ran, when it was last driven and why it came off the road. If those dates are approximate, label them as approximate.

Useful details include whether the battery is disconnected or flat, any visible leaks, warning messages previously recorded, missing components and work that stopped part-way through. Do not describe it as “just a battery” unless that has actually been established.

You do not need to attempt a start solely for photographs or a video. If the original fault involved overheating, abnormal engine noise or loss of oil pressure, ask a specialist before running it again. Existing workshop findings are more useful than a new recording made without knowing whether starting it is appropriate.

Keep the keys, service records and invoices together. They help explain the car's history even when its current condition cannot be fully demonstrated.

## Plan collection around the car and the access

A recovery operator needs more than a postcode. Before a vehicle is dispatched, explain:

- Whether the car can roll, steer and select neutral, or whether this is unknown.
- Whether the parking brake releases and the tyres hold air.
- Any low suspension, damaged bodywork or removed wheels.
- The garage opening, driveway slope and space available for loading.
- Gates, height restrictions, underground parking or access controlled by another person.
- Loose components or spare wheels that are included in the sale.

Photographs of the approach and the car's position can answer questions that a street address cannot. Ask the operator what measurements it needs; do not try to move a stuck vehicle or force a release mechanism without appropriate advice.

If the car is stored at a workshop, agree who will release it and check any outstanding balance before booking collection. Keep the garage's contact and opening hours available for the arrangements.

## Should you pay for an MOT before selling?

There is no single answer. If the car is otherwise usable, a current test may resolve some buyer questions. If a major fault already prevents use, arranging transport, diagnosis and repairs solely to obtain a test may add a commitment you do not want.

An MOT is not a quotation for correcting every fault. Compare the proposed work with an offer for the car in its present condition before deciding. Our [repair-or-sell guide](/blog/supercar-engine-failure-repair-or-sell) explains how to compare costs without assuming that every pound spent will be recovered in the sale.

## Notify DVLA and keep the handover record

Selling a SORN vehicle still requires the appropriate [DVLA sale or transfer notification](https://www.gov.uk/sold-bought-vehicle). Follow the route for the actual purchaser, whether that is a private keeper or a motor trader. Keep the confirmation with the receipt and agreed sale details.

Your SORN does not transfer to a new keeper. A private buyer keeping the vehicle off the road must make their own declaration; motor traders can have different requirements. Tax does not transfer with a sale either.

If you do not have the V5C, explain that before collection. DVLA's linked guidance provides a postal notification process for a sale without a log book; the normal online service cannot be used without one. Agree what documentation the buyer needs in advance.

## Ask for an offer before recommissioning the car

You can [request a valuation](/#valuation) with the registration, mileage, postcode and known faults. Tell us it is SORN, whether the MOT has expired and how long it has been off the road.

We buy directly and also work with specialist buyers. Collection arrangements are agreed around the car's condition and access. Confirm the final offer and any inspection conditions before proceeding, and ensure the agreed payment has cleared before the car leaves.
