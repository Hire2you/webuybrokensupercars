An auction estimate is higher than a specialist's offer. Does that make the auction the better choice? It might, but first establish what each figure represents and what you would receive after the sale.

An estimate is an expectation, a reserve is a selling threshold under the auction's terms, and a purchase offer may still depend on inspection. Comparing them as if they are three guaranteed amounts can lead to an expensive misunderstanding.

For a broken supercar, the most useful comparison combines the expected proceeds with the work, uncertainty and collection arrangements involved.

## First check that the venue suits the car

An auction specialising in usable collector cars and one handling damaged vehicles may attract different buyers. Ask whether the venue accepts your exact condition: a non-runner, an incomplete repair, accident damage or a confirmed insurance category.

Do this before paying for preparation or transporting the car. Send a description of the fault, current photographs and any workshop report. Ask what evidence the auction needs and how it will describe unresolved problems in the listing.

An auction can be worth considering when there is a suitable audience and you are comfortable letting bidding establish the price. A specialist purchase offer may suit you if you prefer to agree the amount and handover arrangements directly. Neither route guarantees the highest return for every car.

## Work out what reaches your bank account

For an auction, start with a realistic possible selling price and subtract the charges payable by you. Request a written schedule covering sale fees, any VAT on charges, transport, preparation, storage and optional services. Some may not apply; establish which ones do.

[Copart's published seller-fee page](https://www.copart.co.uk/Content/uk/en/auction-seller-fees), for example, lists a sale fee and separate services including collection, reserves, re-runs and storage. That is a reason to check the complete quotation, not to assume all auctions use the same charging model. Confirm the current terms for your vehicle before entering it.

Keep seller charges separate from a buyer's premium. The latter is paid by the purchaser under the auction's terms; it is not automatically an additional deduction from your proceeds. It can still matter to what a bidder is prepared to spend overall.

For a direct offer, ask whether the stated figure is the amount you receive, whether collection is included and what circumstances could change it. Compare like with like: a completed inspection and agreed price provide more certainty than an initial online estimate.

## Understand the reserve and an unsold result

A reserve can prevent a sale below an agreed threshold, subject to the venue's terms. It cannot make bidders reach that threshold. Ask what happens if the vehicle does not sell and whether you will be approached about a lower bid.

Before committing, establish any re-entry, storage, withdrawal or return-transport costs. Find out whether the vehicle can be offered elsewhere during the listing period. These questions matter particularly if the car is already at a storage site charging by the day.

Do not agree to a no-reserve listing unless you understand and accept the possibility of a lower result than you hoped for. Equally, do not assume an ambitious reserve is evidence of the car's market value.

## Check who handles payment and collection

Auction services differ in what happens after bidding ends. For example, [Collecting Cars explains that payment and collection arrangements are made after the successful buyer is connected with the seller](https://collectingcars.com/guides/how-auctions-work/sell-with-us). Other operators handle more of the handover themselves.

Ask your chosen venue when you receive cleared funds, who holds the car in the meantime and what happens if the buyer does not complete. A successful bid and money received are different stages.

With a specialist purchase, agree who the contracting buyer is and who is collecting on its behalf. Get the payment and release arrangements in writing. If you use our service, the agreed payment must be cleared before the vehicle leaves.

For either route, tell the transporter about low clearance, locked wheels, dismantled components or restricted access. Our [SORN and non-runner collection guide](/blog/sell-sorn-supercar-without-mot) covers the practical information to provide.

## Give both routes the same evidence

You cannot compare offers fairly if one buyer has seen the diagnostic report and another has only seen a flattering photograph. Prepare one accurate description and use it consistently.

Include the registration, mileage, specification, service history, known faults, modifications and any missing parts. Identify what is confirmed and what is still being investigated. Photograph damage clearly and include the interior, wheels and current storage position.

For an insurance write-off, provide the confirmed category and repair status. Our [Category S and Category N guide](/blog/sell-category-s-n-supercar) explains the evidence and insurance arrangements to resolve before a sale.

## Choose according to your priorities

An auction deserves consideration if the venue has a suitable audience, the terms work for you and you can accommodate the listing and payment timetable. Exposure to competing bidders may be valuable, but assess the likely result after costs rather than the highest comparable result you can find.

A specialist buyer deserves consideration if it can assess the fault, make a clear offer and agree a collection that fits your situation. Ask the same detailed questions about conditions and payment that you would ask an auctioneer.

If you need the garage space by a particular date, tell both parties. A higher possible price may be less useful than a confirmed arrangement, but that is your trade-off to make.
